Paste a genome and a target gene inside it. The designer suggests a forward primer, a reverse primer and a probe whose melting temperatures suit your qPCR's annealing step, rejects any whose oligos fold into hairpins or stick together as primer dimers, ranks them, and checks with the specificity model that each design makes only one product in the whole genome.

Why temperature decides which primers work
95 °C · 3 sDenaturation. The double-stranded DNA, including last cycle's product, melts apart into single strands.
55 °C · 30 sAnnealing and extension. Primers and probe bind their sites, the polymerase copies, and the probe is cut, which gives the signal.
  • Primers must hold at the annealing step. Their Tm must be 6–13 °C above it: every CDC primer is at least 6.6 °C above 55 °C. Closer, and part of each cycle's template goes uncopied. Far above, and a primer also holds on to sites with mismatches.
  • Both primers alike. One annealing temperature serves both, so their Tms must be within 4 °C.
  • Probe above the primers. The probe must already sit on the template when the primer is extended through it, so its Tm must be at least 2 °C above the higher primer (CDC probes: 2.6–6.3 °C).
  • The product must unwind at 95 °C. A long or GC-rich product melts too high to separate; its Tm must stay at or below 90 °C.
  • Why a higher annealing temperature gives fewer designs. Raising the annealing temperature raises the Tm every primer must reach (annealing + 6 °C) and the probe's with it. Primers here are at most 24 bases and 40–60 % GC, so their Tm can only go so high, and fewer stretches of the gene can reach it. A primer that cannot would spend much of each cycle unbound, copying less template every round. On the SARS-CoV-2 N gene (about 47 % GC):

Each target has a best range, high enough for specificity and low enough that its sequence can reach the Tms; for this gene it is about 55 °C, the CDC's setting. AT-rich targets reach lower Tms, so they run out of designs at lower annealing temperatures.
  • No primer dimers. Oligos in the same tube can stick to each other instead of the template. If a primer's 3′ end is paired, the polymerase extends it into a short primer-dimer product that uses up primers. Every pair of oligos (and each with itself) is lined up at every offset, and the paired region's stacking energy is summed, including a mismatch stacked on either end of it. A design is skipped if any dimer is more stable than −9 kcal/mol at 37 °C, or one with a primer's 3′ end paired is more stable than −5 (the probe's 3′ end is blocked, so it does not count). The CDC assays' worst are −5.9 and −3.1.
  • No hairpins. An oligo can fold back on itself: a stretch pairs with another stretch of the same oligo, closing a loop of at least 3 bases. A folded primer cannot bind, and if its 3′ end is in the stem it can prime itself. The energy adds the stem's stacking, the loop penalty and the mismatch where the loop closes (SantaLucia & Hicks 2004). A design is skipped if any oligo folds more stably than −3 kcal/mol at 37 °C, or a primer with its 3′ end in the stem more stably than −2. The CDC assays' most stable hairpin is −1.0.
  • Tm is computed with the second model: nearest-neighbor energies (SantaLucia 1998), with salt and Mg²⁺ corrections as in Primer3, at the reaction conditions you set (default 50 mM K⁺, 3 mM Mg²⁺, 0.8 mM dNTPs, 500 nM each primer and 125 nM probe, the CDC amounts). More Mg²⁺ or more oligo raises every Tm. At Primer-BLAST's default conditions it gives the same Tm as Primer-BLAST.
How the designer chooses
  • Primers are 18–24 bases, the probe 18–30. Every primer and probe must be 40–60 % GC.
  • At each position, the length whose Tm is closest to the target (primers: annealing + 8 °C; probe: annealing + 15 °C). Primers outside annealing + 6 to + 13 °C are not used.
  • The probe sits in the middle of the gap between the primers, among probes at least 2 °C above both primers. Primer pairs more than 4 °C apart, and products above 90 °C, are skipped.
  • Ranking, best first: penalty = each oligo's distance from its target Tm (°C) + (product length − shortest allowed) ÷ 10. Lower is better.
  • The target's GC is checked first: above 70 % stops the design, 60–70 % or below 40 % gives a warning. An AT-rich target may give no design, because its 40–60 % GC stretches melt too low.
  • Designs whose oligos form a hairpin or a primer dimer (see above) are skipped.
  • A design is kept only if each oligo binds exactly once in the whole genome and the first model finds exactly one product, with the probe inside it. Later designs do not overlap earlier ones.
  • Not considered yet: hairpin stems with mismatches or bulges, and RNA folding of the virus genome at the binding site. Every design here still needs lab testing (standard curve, melt curve, no-template and specificity controls) before it can be trusted.
